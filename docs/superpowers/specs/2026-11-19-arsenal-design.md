# Arsenal

## Why

The owner said that build variety matters a great deal. There are seven classes of machine, 133
parts and seven places to keep a build, and a contract can be flown in whichever build suits it.
What decides how a build *plays*, though, is its weapons, and the weapons differ in six small ways:
two that fire more than once, one that fires at everything, one that goes through armour, one that
heats its target and one that takes its energy. Each of those is a thing the weapon does to the
other side. None of them asks the pilot to fly their own machine any differently.

This round adds five kinds of weapon that do. Each makes something the pilot already has (heat,
energy left over, an enemy off balance, enemies standing together, a long fight) worth building
round and playing towards.

## Goals

1. **Five new kinds of weapon**, each of which changes what a good turn looks like in the machine
   that carries it.
2. **Ten weapons** that carry them, two of each kind: one to be had by the middle of the story and
   one for a pilot deep into the arena. Three of the ten are on a shelf as soon as that client's
   first contract is done, so a new game meets them early.
3. **Seen and read where the pilot decides.** The part's own line in the shop and the garage, the
   weapon's row in a battle and the aiming line all say what the weapon will do *now*.
4. **Flown properly by the game itself**: Auto Turn, a sparring partner in a saved build, the mirror
   match and the odds a briefing gives all use the new kinds the way a pilot would.
5. An APK after every step.

## Not goals

- No new class of machine. How a build is classed does not change, and none of the new kinds
  decides it.
- No new slot, no second shoulder mount.
- No enemy in the story, the arena or on the board is given one of the new weapons: every fight
  that exists is the fight it was. (A sparring partner or the mirror carries whatever the pilot's
  own build does.)
- No change to any weapon that exists, and none to a number of the rules of combat.
- No drones or anything else that acts by itself. (That may be a round of its own.)

## What would make it a failure

- A weapon that is simply the best: fitted to every build, whatever the contract. Each new kind has
  a build it belongs in and is ordinary, or worse, outside it.
- A weapon nobody would buy: one that takes a build made for it to be as good as what the pilot
  had already.
- A kind the pilot cannot understand from one line and one battle.
- A fight that exists becoming easier or harder for a pilot who buys none of them.
- The game flying them badly, so that the odds at a briefing are wrong for a build that carries one.

## The five kinds

### Redline

*The hotter the machine runs, the harder it hits.*

A redline weapon does more damage for the heat its own machine carries when it fires: a set
percentage for every ten points, up to the limit of a hundred. Fired cold it is a little worse than
an ordinary weapon of its price; fired hot it is a good deal better.

What it changes: the order of a turn (the weapons that heat the machine first, this one last), how
close to the limit the pilot dares to stand, and what a generator's cooling is worth. A generator
that cools badly has been only a cost until now.

### Surge

*It takes all the energy the machine has left.*

A surge weapon has no fixed cost. It asks a least amount to fire at all, takes everything that is
left, and does its damage for every hundred points of energy it took, and pushes as hard. Its heat
is the same whatever it took.

What it changes: every other thing a turn's energy can buy (a boost, a guard, another weapon) is
weighed against it, to the last point, and the size of a generator becomes a weapon's strength.
It is the natural last shot of a turn.

In a battle against ordinary machines a volley that destroys something gives the energy it took
back. A surge weapon is given back only the least it asks, not all it took: the first shot of a
turn is the great one.

### Punish

*Half as much again against a machine that is off balance.*

Every weapon does more against a staggered machine. A punish weapon does more still, by a set
percentage on top of that. It pushes little itself.

What it changes: a build of two parts, one weapon that knocks a machine off balance and one that is
fired at it while it reels, and the order they are fired in.

### Fork

*A hit leaps on to a second machine.*

When a fork weapon hits, the bolt goes on to one more enemy: the next one along the line, as the
targets are gone through. It strikes that one's core for a set share of what the weapon does,
without a roll to hit. A miss leaps nowhere, and so does a hit when nothing else stands.

What it changes: against a crowd one trigger does the work of one and a half, and the second
machine cannot dodge it; against a single machine it is an ordinary weapon and a little dear.

### Spool

*A shot more for every turn in a row it fires.*

A spool weapon fires two shots the first turn, and one more for every turn in a row it has been
fired since, up to a set number. A turn in which it is not fired (the machine overheated, was out
of energy or fired something else) brings it back to two.

What it changes: it is poor in a short fight and very good in a long one, so it is a weapon to
bring against one large machine, and it asks for a generator that keeps the heat down turn after
turn, where a redline asks for the opposite.

## Where they are seen

- **The part's line** in the shop and the garage, one line for each kind, like the lines the six
  kinds have now. A surge weapon's energy reads as the least it asks, with a mark that it takes more.
- **The weapon's row in a battle** says what the weapon would do this turn: the energy a surge would
  take, how many shots a spool has wound up to, how much a redline's heat adds.
- **The aiming line** already shows the damage a hit would do, and does for these: it is worked out
  from the heat, the energy and the balance as they stand. For a fork it also names where the bolt
  goes on to.
- **On the field**: a fork's bolt is drawn leaping from the first machine to the second. A word
  rises where a kind did its work (a redline fired hot, a surge, a punish landing on a reeling
  machine), as OVERKILL does now.
- **The manual** gets a chapter, the guide and the README a section.

## The weapons

Two of each kind. The numbers are the builder's to set by measuring (see below); what is fixed here
is what each is and where it comes from.

| Kind | Early or middle | Deep in the arena |
|---|---|---|
| Redline | a hand weapon from the Undertow | a shoulder weapon |
| Surge | a hand weapon from Lumen | a shoulder weapon |
| Punish | a hand gun from Hadron | a blade |
| Fork | a hand weapon from Lumen | a shoulder weapon |
| Spool | a hand weapon from Hadron | a shoulder weapon |

One hand weapon from each of the three clients is on the shelf once that client's first contract is
done. The rest come with the exclusive contract, with ranks of the arena, and one with the audit's
end. None is built to order: the Gauntlet offers them as it offers anything the market sells.

Each has a picture of its own, drawn the way the other weapons' were.

## How the game flies them

The automatic pilot (Auto Turn, the odds, a sparring partner, the mirror) fires in an order that
suits what it carries: whatever staggers before a punish weapon, whatever heats before a redline
weapon, and a surge weapon last of all, after it has taken its stance if there is energy for both.
It does not vent heat it is about to use, and it does not fire a fork at a machine beside one the
client wants in one piece when the leap could finish that one.

## Measuring

The balance of what exists is measured by flying every contract many times in builds a pilot could
afford at each stage. The new weapons are measured the same way, each in two builds: one made for
it, and an ordinary one in which it only replaces a weapon of its price.

- In the ordinary build it wins no more often than the weapon it replaced, give or take a few
  points in a hundred.
- In the build made for it, it wins more often than that build does with the best weapon there was
  before, in the kind of fight it is for; and not in every kind of fight.
- No test of the balance of what exists changes.

## Approaches considered

For what to add: more weapons of the six kinds there are (cheap, and no new way to play); new
classes of machine (the classes are held by the arena's seven masters, the stripes and the saved
builds: an eighth would be a round of its own and would still need weapons that play differently);
or new kinds of weapon inside the classes there are (chosen).

For which kinds: the ten that were weighed are in the plan's notes. The five chosen are those that
change what the pilot does with their own machine and that the rules can state in a line. A weapon
that fires back while guarding and a drone that fires by itself were left out: both have the
machine act outside its own turn, which the battle's screen has never had to show.

For a surge and a kill that gives energy back: all of it back (one trigger could clear a field at
full strength, every time); none of it (the one weapon to which a kill gives nothing); or the
least it asks (chosen: the weapon is ready again, as every weapon is, and the great shot is still
one a turn).

For where a fork leaps: the weakest other machine (best for the pilot, hardest to foresee); one
the pilot picks (a second aiming step for half a shot); or the next along the line (chosen: it can
be seen before the trigger is pulled, and said in a word).

## Constraints

- Every new text has its Dutch, inside the room its kind has.
- Pictures are made at the lowest quality, and the round's share of the image budget is small: ten
  weapons, well under a dollar.
- A kept game from before the round opens as it was.
- Nothing is merged without the owner's word.

## Left to the builder

Every number; the names; which rank of the arena opens which; the words; whether there is a medal;
how the row in a battle says what it says within the width it has.
