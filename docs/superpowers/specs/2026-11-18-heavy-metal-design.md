# Heavy Metal: design

## Why

The user asked how the game does on power fantasy, and called it very important in a mech game.
Measured, it does badly. The game is a fair fight from the first contract to the last, and a mech
game also has to let a pilot who has built something frightening feel that it is.

Measured with the automatic player in the best frame the shop sells, every perk that counts in a
fight and the bench at its limit, over every story contract and the patrol (twelve seeds each):

| | End of a first telling | At the peak (third telling) |
|---|---|---|
| Rounds a battle | 4.1 | 6.0 |
| Volleys fired for each machine destroyed | 3.8 | 5.0 |
| Turns of the pilot's in which a machine is destroyed | 43% | 31% |
| Turns in which two are destroyed | 2% | 1% |
| Turns in which three are destroyed | never | never |

On the patrol, which never changes (two recon drones), the same pilot takes 2.7 rounds.

So: the strongest pilot there can be never clears a field in a turn, the weakest machines in the
game take it most of three rounds, and a pilot gets weaker against the field as the tellings go on
(enemies half again as tough at the peak, the pilot a quarter more at best). What the game has for
the feeling (the Overdrive, staggering, shooting parts off, the camera) makes for drama; it does
not make for being overwhelming.

## Goals

- **Momentum.** Destroying a machine pays the pilot back at once: the weapon that did it is ready
  again. A pilot who outguns the field goes through it in one turn; a machine that takes more than
  one volley stops the run.
- **Shatter.** A heavy weapon goes through a light machine: one blow that takes half of its core
  finishes it. Momentum alone moved the figures above by almost nothing (a machine that takes two
  volleys never starts a run); this is what makes a kill a matter of one volley.
- **They break.** A pilot who destroys two machines in one turn is seen doing it: whatever ordinary
  machine is left breaks off rather than face the next turn.
- **Swarms.** Contracts made for it: more light machines than the field holds, arriving as others fall.
- **A name.** What the pilot has done earns a name that the game uses: on the pilot's page, at the
  head of a battle, and in what is said about them.
- **Weight.** A kill that is far more than was needed looks and sounds like it; a run of kills is
  counted on the screen.
- The same measurements at the end of the round, in the plan, beside the ones above.

## Not goals

- No new parts, frames or bosses, and no change to what a part costs or does.
- No change to how strong a cycle's enemies are (the round before settled that) or to the bench.
- No new progression to manage (no second perk tree, no new currency).
- No real-time action: it stays a game of turns.

## What would make it a failure

- A hard fight that is no longer hard. A fight against a boss, a named pilot, an elite, an
  objective's target or one machine must be the fight it was. The feeling has to come from the
  fights a pilot has outgrown, not from the ones that are the point.
- A rule the player cannot see coming: a weapon that fires again, or a machine that leaves, without
  the screen saying why.
- A first hour that is harder, or that a new pilot cannot follow. (As first written this said the
  first hour must not play differently at all. It does: the starter's rifle goes through a recon
  drone, so the first battle teaches the rule. What is held to is that the first act is won as
  often as it was, and that the screen says what happened.)
- Lines of the story lost. What is said when one enemy is left is sometimes a clue; a rule that
  ends a battle the moment two machines fall would skip it.
- Noise. On a handheld, a counter, a word and a heavier bang; nothing that covers the field or
  ignores the calm setting.

## How it works

**Open battles.** A battle is *open* when it begins against more than one machine and none of
them *leads*. A machine leads if it has phases (a boss), a name and a face, the mark of an elite,
if the objective names it (a leader, one to disable, one to drive off), or if it is a Bailiff, the
one machine that commands others without being any of those. Whether a battle is open is settled
when it begins and does not change. The enemies of an open battle are *ordinary*, those that join
later included. Everything this round adds to a battle is a rule of an open battle and of no
other: a fight that is not open is, shot for shot, the fight it was before. A drill is never an
open battle, whatever is in it: it teaches one thing, in a mech and against machines picked so
that doing that thing wins it.

**Momentum.** In an open battle, when a volley destroys a unit, the weapon that fired it is ready
again in the same turn and the energy it took is given back. Heat is not given back and the round
is spent, so a run ends in an overheat or an empty magazine if nothing else stops it: pushing on
is a choice. A charged weapon has to be charged again. Each unit counts the units its volleys have
destroyed this turn (its *chain*); the battle says so in an event the screen and the sound can
use, once for a volley, when all of it has landed, and not at all if the volley ended the battle.
The rule is the same for every unit; only a pilot has more than one thing to shoot at. The screen
says of the weapon what is true of it (ready again; can charge again; empty; a blade that needs
Boost again; nothing left standing to turn it on), and says it as the pilot is handed the turn
again, not before: nothing waits for it.

**Shatter.** In an open battle, a hit that takes at least half of an ordinary machine's core, as
that core stood at full strength, takes all that is left of it. It is measured on what arrives:
after the armour's resistance, a brace, a stagger. The aiming line says DESTROYS in place of the
figure wherever one hit would finish the machine, by shattering it or by being enough. The pilot's
own mech is never shattered.

**They break.** When a unit's chain reaches two, every ordinary enemy still standing is shaken.
(The chain counts what volleys destroy: a module's blast and an ally's fire are not in it.) A
shaken machine is out of the fight when its own turn comes, instead of acting: one that can move
leaves the field, one that cannot (a turret, a pylon, a mast) shuts down where it stands, and the
battle goes on without it (or is won, if nothing else stands). Until then it can still be shot,
and it says SHAKEN over its head and nothing else: a bomb that has lost its nerve leaves with its
fuse unlit. A machine that left is not a kill. Breaking off at its own turn, not at once, is what
keeps the line that is said when one enemy is left.

**Swarms.** A battle can have a reserve: machines that are not on the field yet. At the start of
each round, after what stood has been counted against anything that is being protected, as many
of them come on as there is room for, in order. The battle is won when nothing stands and nothing
is left to come. The briefing and the field say how many are still to come, the field as far as
the message bar has said it; machines that come on together are said in one line. One swarm is on
offer beside the open board at all times, a new one after every battle won: light machines only
(never more than two gunships), six to nine in all, three on the field and the rest behind them,
paid by the head. (As first written
there were to be swarm contracts on the board and a standing job in the side work. One contract
that is always there and always new is both.)

**A name.** The record a game already keeps is read as renown: one for every machine destroyed,
eight for a boss beaten, four for a rank of the arena, forty for a telling of the story. Renown is
read as one of five names, at 20, 60, 150, 320 and 600: Line Item, Write-Off, Total Loss, Act of
God, The Last Invoice, each with a line that says what it means to the other side. No new saved
state: the name is worked out from the record, and nothing on the record ever goes down. A name is
announced as it is earned, as a medal is; a game loaded with one has it without ceremony. The
pilot's record shows it, with what the next asks. The banner that opens a battle carries the
callsign and the name on a band under it, and where the battle is open the message bar opens with
the name and its line: ordinary machines are the ones who have heard it. (As first written it was
to be said where the machines were far below the pilot. Open battles are that.)

**Weight.** A machine destroyed by one blow that carried, past any barrier, at least one and a
half times its whole core as that stood at full strength is an *overkill*: the event says so, the
machine goes up harder (a last blast half as large again, the hardest jolt there is, more thrown
into the air, a slam under the bang), and the word is over it for a moment, clear of the figure of
the hit and drawn over the fire. It is said in every fight and changes nothing. A chain of two or
more is counted over the pilot's mech ("CHAIN x2") with a note that rises with it. A machine that
breaks off leaves under the word for it ("ROUT"). The banner of an open battle names the rule that
holds there ("MOMENTUM") at the end of the line that says what the battle asks, so that a pilot
knows before the first shot which rules the fight is fought by. Under the calm setting the words
are there and the rest is not; none of it holds a turn up; the close look at the last machine of a
battle is left as it was.

(As first written an overkill was twice what was left of the core. Measured that way every
finishing blow on a worn machine was one, and no kill in a swarm ever was, which is the wrong way
round. Measured against the whole core at twice, it was hardly ever seen: of the frames the shop
sells only the fifth ever did it, in two kills of a hundred. At one and a half times the whole core
nothing the first three frames carry is one as the automatic player flies them (an Overdrive can
make one sooner: an early mortar under Deadeye, on a drone), and from the fourth it is up to one
kill in twelve of the story and from one in ten to two in three of the kills in a swarm. At once
the core it was three kills in four, and more, of a grown pilot's swarm, which is no longer a thing
to remark on. It is said of the other side only: the pilot's own end is never called one.)

## Approaches considered

1. **Rules that pay for kills (chosen).** Momentum, Shatter and the break turn a pilot's strength
   into a different kind of turn, not only a shorter one, and need no new thing to manage. They
   are rules of open battles, so the hard fights stay as they are.
2. **More raw power for the pilot** (bigger bench steps, weaker later cycles). Rejected: it is the
   balance the last round measured its way to, it would make the hard fights easy, and a bigger
   number is not a feeling.
3. **A second tree of ace abilities** bought with credits or earned by deeds. Rejected for now: it
   is a system to manage, and the first of its abilities would be Momentum. If the rules above
   want a way to grow later, this is where it would go.
4. **Presentation only** (bigger explosions, bigger numbers). Rejected as the whole answer: the
   measurements say the turns themselves are the problem. It is the last part of this round.

## Constraints

- A saved game from before this round loads and stays valid; nothing new is saved for the name.
- Every new English text has its Dutch, inside the room its kind has.
- Balance tests of the story are measured again and reported; where a hard fight moved by more
  than a few seeds, the rule is changed, not the test. (This is what happened: see below.)
- No image is generated unless a part of the round cannot do without one; low quality only, and
  the ledger's ceiling stands.
- Nothing is merged without the user's word.

## What was measured, and what it changed

The first rule built was Momentum everywhere and Shatter for every machine that did not itself
lead. Played over 120 seeds a contract, that left every fight against one machine exactly as it
was, and made the late contracts with escorts much easier: the third audit contract went from 81
wins in a hundred to 97 for a full Ronin, the second verdict from 76 to 90, and the hardest
contract of the game on Veteran at the peak from 38 to 71. Momentum alone accounted for most of
that: a clerk destroyed beside a Bailiff gave the volley back to be put into the Bailiff. Two
answers were tried and dropped: tougher enemies in those contracts (it holds the win rate of a
heavy build and breaks a light one, which gets no benefit to pay for it), and machines that hold
only while their leader stands (the fight still changes when the leader falls first).

What was kept is the rule above: open battles, settled as a battle begins. With it, over the same
seeds, every verdict and the third audit contract are won exactly as often as before, to the
seed, and the finales (two open battles and a duel) within a few seeds; nine fights that are not
open are kept in the tests by what they come to. What moved is what the round is for. A contract fought against light machines only is won more often by a pilot
who outguns them: the last contract of the Lumen war (bomb crawlers at a core stack) from 92 in a
hundred to 99 for a full Ronin, and from 21 to 86 in the heaviest frame, which used to be the
worst at it; the second audit contract (lien drones at a hangar) from 91 to 98, and from 9 to 95.
The balance test that held the first of those under 98 now says so in its own words.

Swarms (the third part) are where the figures of the first table turn over. In the swarm of their
stage a full Ronin destroys a machine in nine turns of ten, two or more in six and the whole
field in three; a new pilot in the starter frame, two or more in three turns of ten. Before the
round the best pilot there could be did the first in four turns of ten and the second in one of
fifty. Hunter drones were tried in them and taken out: hard to hit, they made a swarm a slog of
twenty rounds and a contract lost one time in three. More crawlers in the last contract of the
Lumen war were tried too: a full Ronin still won it 99 times in a hundred, and the frame before
it lost it more often on Veteran, so that contract is as it was.

The break (the second part) moved no win rate of the story: a field holds three machines, so when
two fall in a turn there is one left to lose its nerve, and the battle it leaves was won already.
It ends one open battle in seven a turn sooner at the end of a first telling, and is there to be
seen. (On the open board it does move one thing, which the review measured: a frame far too light
for its contract wins a battle of one heavy machine and two light ones more often, 27 times in a
hundred where Momentum and Shatter alone made it 14 and the game before made it 6, because the
heavy one leaves. With the frames of the stage it is 0.4 of a point.)

## What the review found, and what it changed

Two reviewers who had not seen the work read the round against this document and the plan: one the
rules, one the screens and the text. The rules held: nearly 58,000 battles played on the version
before and on this one, by the automatic player and by a player who presses at random, came to the
same event, the same die and the same state in every fight that is not open (25,509 of them). What they
found was balance the plan had not measured, sentences that said more than the rules, and things on
the screen that ran ahead of each other.

- **Swarms with gunships.** From the sixth tier a swarm could be seven gunships of nine. On Veteran
  half a gunship's core is more than a lance carries, and the heaviest frame took forty rounds at
  it and lost one battle in five to the clock. A swarm now has two gunships at most. Measured on
  every swarm of that stretch, both difficulties, three tellings: no frame of the stage ever meets
  the clock; a grown pilot takes five to eight rounds; the heaviest frame as it is sold is still
  slow on Veteran (fifteen rounds: it is hot and misses what flies, gunships or no) and wins.
- **Drills.** The drill on bracing was passed without bracing 121 times in 200 (6 before): the
  lent lance went through the bombs and the marksman lost its nerve. A drill is never open now,
  and all eleven play to their last event as they did before.
- **The Gauntlet.** More than half its floors are open battles. A run gets about a floor further
  (9.6 for 8.6 with a second life, 7.9 for 7.35 without), and with a second life the tenth floor
  is reached by half the runs where it was three in eight. That is kept, and pinned in the tests:
  what a pilot who outguns the field can do is true wherever ordinary machines are met.
- **A boss met after an open battle.** The fight is the fight it was from the same state. The
  pilot more often arrives with more left in the magazine, and the contract is won more often:
  the Leviathan's by the second frame 71 times in 120 for 54, by the sixth 119 for 110; the fourth
  Undertow contract by the sixth 106 for 93; the first audit by the fourth 114 for 107 (the
  review's figures, 120 seeds). That is the round working as meant, and is said where the guide
  used to say nothing changed.
- **What the screen said.** "Ready again" was said of a weapon whose last round the kill had been,
  of one that has to charge, with nothing left standing, and twice in the middle of a salvo; and a
  kill handed the turn back six tenths of a second later than in a fight that is not open. It is
  said once now, as it is, and waits for nothing. The strip ran a message ahead of the bar; a
  shaken machine lost its bars a second before it left; a turret "broke off" and slid from the
  field; a word could lie on another word, or behind a blast, or past the edge. All of that is
  as it should be now, and each has its test.
- **Text.** One swarm title was a story contract's title, and none had Dutch. The manual's
  sentences on these rules are each held against the rules in `tests/content/claims.test.ts`.
- **Left as they were, with a reason.** The aiming line says DESTROYS without the word for a
  salvo or the count of a burst: the Dutch line has no room for both, and the promise is the more
  useful of the two. A shaken machine is lit, calmly, in a calm picture, as a staggered one is:
  that is what the calm setting does with a unit that changes state. The strip's "+4" beside an
  objective is reached by no contract of the game and is kept: the rules allow a reserve beside
  something to protect, and the strip must not be silent the day a contract asks for both.

## Left to the builder

The exact wording on the screen, the sound of a chain, how a machine is seen to leave, the ladder
of names and where its steps lie, how many machines a swarm has at which tier, and what a swarm
pays, are settled in the plan as each part is built, with what was measured.
